Frequently asked questions

The questions that arrive most often, grouped by subject. If yours is not here, send it to us. We answer every one, and the good ones end up on this page.

Using the recipes

Metric, throughout. Weights are in grams and kilograms, liquids in millilitres and litres. One cup on this site is 250 ml, one tablespoon is 15 ml and one teaspoon is 5 ml. If you are cooking from a book printed elsewhere, note that a United States cup is 240 ml and an imperial cup is 284 ml, so the same recipe can be out by more than a tablespoon per cup. For flour, sugar and anything else where packing matters, weigh it. The full comparison is on our kitchen basics and conversions page, and why it matters is explained in this article.

The toggle sits in the sticky bar under the header on every recipe page. Switching it converts every ingredient quantity on the page at once, using the 250 ml metric cup. Ingredients that only make sense by weight, such as a whole kolokasi, a piece of pork shoulder or a block of halloumi, stay in grams and kilograms, because a cup measurement of them would tell you nothing.

Yes. Every recipe has a servings stepper above the ingredient list, and changing it recalculates the quantities. Two things it cannot do: it will not resize your tin, and it will not adjust cooking times. As a rough guide, doubling a tray bake wants two trays rather than a longer cook, and halving a cake wants a smaller tin and about a quarter less time. Stews and pulses are the forgiving ones, so start there if you are cooking for a crowd.

Because almost every domestic oven is 10 to 20 °C away from the number on the dial, and the error is not the same across the range. Recipes here give a conventional temperature in °C, the fan equivalent beside it, normally 20 °C lower, and the gas mark where it helps. An oven thermometer costs around €10 in any Cypriot supermarket or homeware shop and tells you what your oven actually does. There is more in our guide to reading your oven.

Usually, and most recipes list the swaps we have actually cooked under a swaps and variations heading. Be more careful with baking, where flour, raising agents, sugar and fat are doing structural work. Swapping self-raising flour for plain, or icing sugar for caster, changes the result noticeably. In savoury cooking you have room: anari stands in for ricotta, pourgouri for rice in most stuffings, and carob syrup for honey if you accept a darker, less floral sweetness.

Yes, and that is a rule rather than an accident. Before a recipe is published we check that its ingredients are on the shelves at Alphamega, Lidl Cyprus, Sklavenitis or Papantoniou, or on a stall at the laiki. Seasonal produce is cheapest and best at the weekly street market: artichokes, broad beans and agrelia in spring, tomatoes, figs and bamies through the summer, pomegranates, quinces and walnuts in autumn, citrus, cauliflower and kolokasi in winter. For the few things the big supermarkets do not carry, such as gochujang, fresh curry leaves or good sushi rice, the Asian and Middle Eastern grocers in Limassol and Nicosia have them. Where an ingredient is genuinely hard to find on the island, the recipe says so and gives a substitute we have tested.

Look for the protected designation of origin mark first, because plenty of cheese sold as grilling cheese is not halloumi and does not behave like it. If you cannot get the real thing, the closest are Turkish hellim, a Greek halloumi-style cheese, or a firm Latin American queso para freír, all of which hold their shape in a hot dry pan. At a push, a very firm feta works in baked dishes but will crumble rather than slice, and a young Cypriot-style anari can replace halloumi only where the cheese is not fried. Paneer keeps its shape but will not brown or squeak, and mozzarella simply melts into a puddle. Whatever you use, taste it before you salt the dish, since halloumi brings a lot of salt with it.

Where a dish can honestly be made nistisimo, the recipe says how, in the swaps section rather than buried in the comments. That means no meat, no dairy and no eggs, and on the stricter days no olive oil and no shellfish either. Plenty of Cypriot cooking is already there: louvi me lahana, black-eyed beans with chard, fasolia, tahini dishes, koupepia made with rice and herbs instead of mince, roast vegetables with lemon, and most of what the laiki sells in March. During Sarakosti and the Easter fast we publish more of them, and the recipe index has filters for vegetarian and vegan that will get you most of the way there.

Dietary needs, nutrition and allergens

They are estimates, calculated from the listed ingredients, and they are labelled as estimates on every recipe. They leave out optional ingredients and anything you serve alongside. Real values move with brands, portion size and substitutions. These figures are general information, not dietary advice, and they are not the regulated nutrition declaration you find on a packet under EU Regulation 1169/2011. See the nutrition and allergen disclaimer.

Treat them as a starting point, not as a clearance. We flag the common allergens present in the recipe as written, following the fourteen that EU Regulation 1169/2011 requires to be declared, but we cannot know which brands you buy or how your kitchen is set up. Packaging sold in Cyprus highlights those allergens in the ingredient list, usually in bold, so read the pack in front of you. If you or someone you cook for has a diagnosed allergy or intolerance, speak to a qualified health professional.

Yes. The recipe index filters for vegetarian, vegan, dairy-free option and gluten-free option. A recipe only carries a dietary tag when it is either already suitable as written or has a specific swap we have cooked and written into the method. Dairy is the harder one in Cypriot cooking, since halloumi, anari and yoghurt turn up everywhere, so those swaps get extra testing before they go up.

No. We are cooks, not dietitians or doctors. You will not find claims about weight loss, detoxing, immunity, healing or any therapeutic effect anywhere on this site. We are not qualified to make them, they would not be true, and EU Regulation 1924/2006 only permits nutrition and health claims that are on the authorised list. For advice about your own diet or health, see a doctor or a registered dietitian.
