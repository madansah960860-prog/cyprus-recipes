Five dinners, one shop: feeding four in Limassol without wasting half the parsley

By Elena Christodoulou. Published 11/08/2026, updated 14/09/2026. About a 14 minute read.

The bin is where most household food money goes. Not the good olive oil, not the fish on Sunday, but the half bunch of parsley, the tired end of a cabbage, the tub of yoghurt bought for one recipe and never opened again. Households across the EU throw out a serious share of what they buy, and Cyprus is no exception. Almost all of it was purchased with good intentions.

The fix is not an app or a spreadsheet. It is a way of choosing five dinners so that the leftovers of one become the beginning of the next. We plan this way every week, it takes about fifteen minutes on a Friday night, and it is built entirely around the Saturday laiki.

Start with the constraint, not the recipes

Most meal planning fails because it starts with a wish list. Five recipes chosen independently, all of them appealing, will want five separate bunches of herbs, three vinegars and two cheeses. You will use a quarter of each.

Start with the actual shape of the week instead.

  • Which nights are genuinely short? Not which nights you wish were short. If Tuesday is football and Thursday runs late, those two dinners must be under thirty minutes or they become a souvlaki from the corner.
  • Which single night has an hour or more? That is where the long cook goes, and it should be one that produces leftovers.
  • Is the oven usable this week? In July nobody is roasting anything. From November the tapsi does half the work. Plan for the season you are actually in.
  • What is already in the fridge and the freezer? Look before you plan, not after you shop.
  • Who is actually eating? Four dinners for four plus one for two is a different shop to five for four.

Only then do you pick recipes, and you pick them to fit the shape you have just described.

The overlap rule

This is the whole method. Every perishable thing you buy should appear in at least two dinners, or in one dinner and one lunch.

Herbs are the obvious case. A bunch of flat leaf parsley is enough for three dishes and turns to slime in five days. So if Monday wants parsley, Wednesday should want it too. If nothing else in the week does, either change a dinner or plan to finish it in a tabbouleh with pourgouri on Friday, which costs almost nothing and uses the stalks as well.

Anchor the week on one long cook

Pick one dish that takes hours and almost no attention, cook it on the day you have time, and make more than you need. A slow-cooked ragù that serves six takes the same twenty minutes of active work whether you make four portions or eight. Two in the freezer is a Friday you never have to think about.

This is where cheap cuts earn their reputation, and in Cyprus that usually means pork. Pork shoulder, pork belly and neck are the best value meat on the island by a wide margin, and all of them are better after two or three hours than after twenty minutes. Beef and lamb here are expensive, so treat them as a Sunday thing. Chicken thighs sit in the middle and never disappoint.

A long cook also gives you a base. A pot of braised pork becomes pasta on Sunday, a filling on Tuesday and a topping for baked potatoes on Thursday, without anyone feeling they are eating leftovers three times.

Where to shop, and what it actually costs

The gap between shops here is real money, and it is largest on exactly the things you buy most of.

  • The laiki is the weekly street market and the cheapest fruit and vegetables on the island, often thirty to forty per cent below the supermarket for anything in season. Growers sell what they picked that week. Go early for the pick of it, or in the last hour when prices drop. Bring cash and your own bags.
  • Lidl Cyprus is generally the cheapest for staples, dairy, tinned goods, flour and frozen vegetables. The range is narrower, which for a planned shop is not a problem.
  • Alphamega is the better full shop: wider range, good fish counter, and its own-brand staples are competitive. Sklavenitis and Papantoniou are worth checking for weekly offers, which are worth planning around when the item is a staple you would buy anyway.
  • The butcher will sell you the cheap cuts the supermarket does not display, cut them how you want, and tell you what is good this week. This is where the biggest savings on protein are.
  • Village stalls and the roadside for oranges near Paphos, potatoes in the Kokkinochoria, walnuts and cherries on the Troodos road. Cheaper again, and fresher.

These figures are indicative. They are what one household paid in Limassol in 2026, not a survey and not a quote. Prices move week to week, they vary between districts, and the same basket in a tourist-area supermarket in August will cost meaningfully more. Use the shape of it, not the numbers.

The lesson in that table is not the total. It is where the money sits. Produce for a family of four is about €26 of it. Meat and fish are €27 for a week of dinners, because most of the week is not built on meat. Cut the protein line and eat pulses twice, and the same week comes in under €75 without anybody noticing.

Buy seasonally, and let the price decide

The clearest signal that something is in season is that it is stacked in crates and it is cheap. If aubergines are €4 a kilo in January, that is the market telling you to cook something else. Our seasonal produce guide lists what is at its best month by month, written for the northern hemisphere, where summer runs from June to August.

Roughly, this is where the cheap food is across the Cypriot year.

  • Spring, March to May. Artichokes, broad beans, agrelia off the field edges, fresh garlic, strawberries, loquats. A kilo of broad beans feeds four for very little.
  • Summer, June to August. Tomatoes, cucumbers, courgettes, aubergines, okra and peppers are at their cheapest of the year. Watermelon is almost free by the kilo. Cook cold, cook on the balcony, and preserve the excess.
  • Autumn, September to November. Grapes during the harvest, pomegranates, quinces, sweet potatoes, walnuts. In November the olive presses run and a tin of new oil is the best value it will be all year.
  • Winter, December to February. Citrus at its cheapest, cauliflower, cabbage, spinach, kolokasi, wild greens and Paphos avocados. A five kilo net of oranges costs about €4 and lasts the week.

The quality argument and the money argument point the same way. A February tomato and an August tomato are not the same product. Cooking to the season is not a virtue exercise, it is how you get better food for less.

Frozen deserves a mention too. Okra, peas, spinach and broad beans are frozen within hours of harvest. Out of season they beat tired fresh produce on both price and taste, and they do not rot in the drawer while you decide what to do with them.

The four habits that cut the most waste

  1. Store herbs like flowers

    Stems in a glass of water, a bag over the top, in the fridge. Parsley and coriander hold for a week and a half instead of four days. Basil is the exception and stays on the bench.

  2. Keep a freezer shelf for halves

    Half a tin of tomatoes, the last of the stock, a chicken carcass, parsley stalks, chilli, grated halloumi. All of it freezes and all of it goes back into a soup or a braise later.

  3. Cook the fragile things first

    Order the week so the softest produce goes earliest. Tomatoes, leaves and figs on Monday and Tuesday. Potatoes, cabbage, kolokasi and pumpkin later, because they will still be fine on Friday.

  4. Leave one night unplanned

    A five night plan across a seven night week leaves room for leftovers, for the night nobody is hungry, and for the night you meant to be organised and were not. Planning all seven guarantees waste.

Putting it together

A worked example from a recent August week. Saturday was the laiki, early, before the heat: three kilos of tomatoes, aubergines, courgettes, peppers, herbs and a watermelon for about €28. Sunday the foukou went on for pork chops, and while the coals burned down the aubergines and peppers for Thursday were charred alongside and put away in oil, which cost nothing extra. Monday was prawn and avocado rice bowls, cold, twenty five minutes, no heat in the kitchen. Tuesday the tomatoes went into a low oven while the pasta water came up, for a pesto pasta salad. Wednesday was fish with a caper dressing, using the parsley bought on Saturday. Thursday was the charred vegetable platter with whipped feta, fifteen minutes because the work was already done.

Five dinners, one market run and one supermarket stop, nothing bought twice, and the only thing that reached the bin was the watermelon rind. The full list for that week, grouped by aisle with the euro costs, is on our meal plans page.

None of this asks you to eat the same five things forever. The method is the constant, not the menu.
