Grams, cups and the 250 ml question: how to read a recipe written anywhere

By Marina Savva. Published 18/08/2026, updated 14/09/2026. About a 12 minute read.

A reader in Nicosia wrote to us about a carrot cake. She had followed an American recipe exactly, weighed nothing, used the measuring cups that came with her mixer, and produced something dense, dry and tight that everyone at the table politely finished. She wanted to know what she had done wrong.

Nothing, really. The recipe said two cups of flour. It meant about 240 grams. She had measured roughly 350. The cake did exactly what physics said it should.

Two separate things had gone wrong at once, and between them they account for most of the failed baking we hear about. Her cup was a different size to the recipe writer's cup, and she had filled it a different way. Neither problem is her fault, and neither is written on the page of any recipe that uses cups.

Why this site is written in grams

Every recipe on Meraki Table is written in metric weight first. Not to be fussy, and not because scales are virtuous. Because a gram is the same in Limassol as it is in Lisbon, and a cup is not.

A recipe is a set of instructions from someone who is not standing in your kitchen. The more precisely the amounts are described, the better your chance of ending up with what they ended up with. Weight is precise. Volume of a dry ingredient is an estimate wearing a number.

Cyprus makes this easy. We are metric and we are in the EU, so the scales in every supermarket and hardware shop read in grams, the packets are labelled in grams, and nobody here grew up measuring butter in sticks. The only reason cups come into it at all is that a great deal of good cooking is published in English by people who use them.

The three cups

There is no single cup. There are three in common circulation, and the word gives you no clue which one a recipe means.

The three cups you will meet in English-language recipes

Metric cup250 mlCyprus, the EU, and most metric countries—

Imperial cup284 mlOlder British and Commonwealth cookbooks14 per cent larger

The metric cup is 250 ml. That is the cup this site means, always, and the one the Cups toggle on every recipe page uses. A quarter of a litre, which is the only sensible way to define a cup in a metric country.

The US cup is 240 ml, or 236 ml if you want the exact customary figure. Four per cent smaller. In a soup, invisible. Across three cups of liquid in a batter it is 30 ml, which is most of a quarter cup you never added.

The imperial cup is 284 ml, because it is half an imperial pint of 568 ml. This is the dangerous one. It is fourteen per cent larger than ours, and it turns up in cookbooks published in Britain, Cyprus and the wider Commonwealth before weight became standard. If you inherited a recipe book from a relative and the quantities are in cups, check the publication date before you trust them.

Spoons are far less of a problem than people fear. A tablespoon is 15 ml, which is three teaspoons, in Cyprus, across the EU, in the United Kingdom and in the United States. A teaspoon is 5 ml essentially everywhere. The one exception still in print is a 20 ml tablespoon of four teaspoons used in some southern-hemisphere books. If you are cooking from one of those, convert any tablespoon of baking powder, bicarbonate of soda, salt or yeast into teaspoons before you start, because those are the ingredients doing chemical work rather than adding flavour, and a third too much of any of them is not recoverable once it is in the bowl.

The bigger problem: a cup of flour is not a fixed amount

Even inside one country, with one cup, volume measurement of flour is unreliable. This causes far more failed baking than the difference between 240 and 250 ml ever will.

Try it. Take a 250 ml cup measure, dip it into the flour bag, press down slightly as you scoop, and level it off. Weigh it. Now spoon flour loosely into the same cup, level it with the back of a knife, and weigh that.

The scooped cup will typically weigh 170 to 180 g. The spooned cup will weigh around 150 g. That is a difference of nearly twenty per cent, from the same flour, in the same cup, measured by the same person two minutes apart. Flour is compressible, and how hard you push is not something a recipe can specify.

Now stack that on top of the cup problem, which is what happened to the carrot cake. An American recipe assumes a spooned US cup of about 120 g. Scooped hard into a 250 ml metric cup you are at 175 g. Two cups: 240 g intended, 350 g delivered. Nearly half as much flour again. No amount of careful creaming survives that.

This is why every baking recipe on this site is written in grams first, and why the cups toggle assumes flour is spooned and levelled rather than scooped. It is also why a set of digital scales, about €15 at Stephanis, does more for your baking than any other purchase at any price.

The one exception worth knowing

Liquids are genuinely fine by volume. Water, milk, oil, stock, wine and juice all measure accurately in a jug, because you cannot compress or aerate them the way you can flour. If a recipe is entirely liquid measures and whole eggs, cups are perfectly good and scales add nothing.

It is dry ingredients where volume falls apart, and specifically the light, compressible ones: flour, icing sugar, cocoa, desiccated coconut, oats, pourgouri and grated cheese. Granulated and caster sugar are surprisingly consistent, because the crystals do not compact much, which is why sugar in cups causes far fewer problems than flour in cups.

How to read a recipe written abroad

Five minutes before you start, not halfway through with wet hands.

  1. Work out where it is from. Spelling gives it away fast. Cilantro, all purpose flour, baking soda, broiler, zucchini and eggplant mean American, so the cup is 240 ml. Coriander, plain flour, bicarbonate of soda, grill, courgette and aubergine mean British or European. A book printed before about 1980 in Britain or Cyprus may well mean the 284 ml imperial cup.
  2. Look for grams first. Most decent sites now publish weight alongside cups, often behind a small metric toggle. If the weights are there, use them and stop thinking about the rest of this list.
  3. If there are no grams, convert the flour yourself. Treat an American cup of plain flour as 120 g and a metric cup as 150 g. Weigh that. Every other problem on this page disappears at this step.
  4. Convert every tablespoon of anything powerful to teaspoons. Baking powder, bicarbonate of soda, salt, yeast, strong spices. Three teaspoons to the tablespoon, and the teaspoon is the same everywhere.
  5. Use a jug for liquids. A measuring jug is more accurate than counting spoons for anything over about 30 ml, and faster.
  6. Check the oven line too. An American recipe is in Fahrenheit for a static oven. 350°F is 180°C conventional, which is 160°C in a fan oven. Converting the temperature but forgetting the fan is a common way to overcook something badly. Our oven guide covers that in full.

Going the other way, from a recipe written here to an American kitchen, the same logic runs in reverse. Our cup is 250 ml, so four of our cups is a fraction over four and a sixth of theirs. Which is precisely the sort of sentence that explains why we publish in grams.

What we do

Every recipe on Meraki Table is written in metric, weighed wherever weight matters. The Metric and Cups toggle in the utility bar on each recipe page converts the quantities to the 250 ml metric cup and the 15 ml tablespoon, using the ingredient weights listed on our kitchen basics and conversions page. Oven temperatures are given as conventional with the fan equivalent and the gas mark beside them.

None of this is about precision for its own sake. It is about removing the variables that stand between you and the thing the recipe writer actually made.

The reader in Nicosia made the carrot cake again, weighing 240 g of flour. She reported back that the second one did not last the evening.
