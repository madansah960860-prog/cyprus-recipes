Modern Cypriot

Village food cooked in flats above Limassol traffic. The recipes have not changed much in eighty years. The kitchens have. This is what the island eats now, written down the way it is actually made.

Village recipes, city kitchens

Almost every Cypriot cook is one or two generations from a village. The recipes came down from a yiayia who had a wood oven, a courtyard, a vine over the door and whatever the season gave her. Most of us now have four induction rings, a balcony and an Alphamega ten minutes away. Modern Cypriot cooking is the negotiation between those two kitchens.

It is not fusion and it is not nostalgia. It is afelia made with pork from the butcher rather than from the family pig, koupepia rolled on a Saturday because the vine leaves were good that week, trachanas from a packet in February because nobody has a flat roof to dry it on any more. The dish survives. The logistics move.

What holds it together is a small set of flavours that turn up everywhere: pork and coriander seed, red wine, lemon, dried oregano and wild thyme, cinnamon in savoury food, tomato cooked down slowly, olive oil used with both hands. Learn those and most of the island opens up.

The other constant is quantity. Cypriot cooking assumes more people will arrive than were invited, and it is built that way. Nothing here is plated for one.

Meze is not a section of the menu. It is a decision about how long the afternoon is going to be.

A proper meze arrives in waves and nobody announces the order. Olives, village bread and tahini first, because they are already in the house. Then the cold plates: taramosalata, beetroot with garlic, koupepia at room temperature, pickled cauliflower gone pink from the juice. Then halloumi off the grill, sheftalia, loukanika, and only after that the things that took the longest, the afelia and the stifado and the octopus in red wine.

Twenty small dishes is normal in a taverna. At home, six is a meze. The principle is the same either way: small plates, long gaps, everybody reaching across.

Halloumi, anari, and what the island does with milk

Halloumi is the ingredient the rest of the world knows, and most of the rest of the world uses it for one thing. It is a brined sheep and goat cheese, traditionally folded around dried mint, and it is not only a grilling cheese. Cypriots grate the hard aged blocks over pasta, fry thin slices for breakfast with watermelon in July, eat it raw in salad, and drop cubes into a lentil soup near the end.

Buy the ones with sheep and goat milk listed first. Cow-heavy industrial halloumi squeaks the same but goes rubbery in the pan and weeps salt water. If a block tastes very salty, soak it in cold water for twenty minutes and dry it well before it goes near heat.

Anari is the other half of the story and it deserves more attention than it gets. It is the fresh whey cheese made straight after the halloumi, soft and mild and faintly sweet, something like a ricotta with less acidity. Fresh anari goes on bread with carob syrup for breakfast, into pastries, into a bowl with honey and cinnamon for a five-minute dessert. The dried salted version is grated over pasta and is one of the great cheap luxuries in a Cypriot fridge.

Frying halloumi without the rubber

Dry the slices properly, use a pan that is hot but not smoking, and turn once. Two minutes a side is usually the whole job. Take it off while it still gives under a finger, because it firms up on the plate, and squeeze lemon over it the second it lands. Halloumi that has sat around will not come back, so cook it last and carry it straight to the table.

Four kitchens in one pantry

Cyprus sits where Greek, Turkish, Levantine and British food all pressed against each other, and the kitchen kept whatever was useful. It is pointless to try to separate the strands and slightly dishonest to claim any one of them alone.

From the Greek side come the olive oil, the oregano, the lemon, the phyllo and the shape of a taverna meal. From the Turkish and Ottoman side come cinnamon and allspice in minced meat, stuffed vegetables, grilled kebabs, syrup-soaked sweets and half the words we use for them. From the wider Levant come tahini, bulgur, pulses cooked plain and dressed hard, and the habit of eating a table of small cold plates before anything hot arrives.

The British decades left less in the food and more in the habits: tea with milk in some houses, hot cross buns in the bakeries at Easter, roast lunches. It is the lightest layer, but it is there, and pretending otherwise makes the story tidier than it is.

What it all means in practice is that a Cypriot pantry holds oregano and cumin, red wine vinegar and pomegranate molasses, feta and tahini, and nobody in the house finds that strange.

Fire, and the foukou

The foukou is a long narrow charcoal trough with a motorised spit across the top, and it stands in more Cypriot gardens and on more balconies than not. It exists for one job: souvla, large pieces of pork shoulder or lamb on a skewer, turning slowly over coals that have burnt down to grey, for an hour and a half or two.

Everything about it is the opposite of a quick grill. The meat goes on in big pieces, seasoned with salt, pepper and sometimes a little dried oregano, and nothing else. The coals are never at full heat under it. Nobody stands over it with tongs, because the spit does the turning, so the cook drifts between the fire and the table for two hours. That drifting is as much the point as the meat.

Charcoal, always. Gas makes a perfectly good weeknight dinner and it does not make souvla, because what you are after is the smoke and the slow render of fat down the length of the skewer. Our BBQ and outdoor collection goes into the fire management properly.

Judging coals without a thermometer

Hold your palm about fifteen centimetres above the grate. If you have to pull it away inside two seconds, the fire is for halloumi, sheftalia and anything thin. Four to six seconds is souvla heat, and that is where a whole shoulder wants to sit. If you can hold it there past eight seconds the fire is finished, and it is time to eat.

Wild greens and the laiki

Cypriot cooking is far more vegetable-led than its reputation suggests, and the reason is the laiki, the weekly street market, plus the older habit of going out and picking. In spring people still walk the scrub for agrelia, the thin wild asparagus, and for the mixed wild greens that get boiled and dressed with olive oil and lemon and called simply horta.

The laiki teaches the same lesson every week: buy whatever there is a mountain of. In March that is artichokes, broad beans, fresh garlic and green almonds. In July it is tomatoes you can smell from a metre away, cucumbers, watermelon, okra and figs. In October it is grapes, pomegranates, quinces, walnuts and the first carobs. In January it is citrus, cauliflower, kolokasi and greens again.

Go early if you want the choice and late if you want the price to come down. Take your own bags, take coins, and do not settle the menu until you have walked the length of it once.
