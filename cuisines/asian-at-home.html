Asian at Home

Asia is not a cuisine. What this collection actually is: a handful of dishes we cook on weeknights, drawn mostly from Chinese, Vietnamese and Thai home cooking, written honestly as a Cypriot home cook's version rather than as the real thing.

A note on what this page is and is not

Cyprus has large Filipino, Sri Lankan, Indian, Vietnamese, Chinese and Nepali communities, and the best cooking of these cuisines on the island is being done by people who grew up with it, mostly in their own kitchens. We are not going to pretend otherwise, and we are not going to publish a recipe titled as authentic when it is a Limassol kitchen's approximation.

So the recipes here are labelled for what they are. Our chicken, ginger and rice noodle soup is not pho. Pho is a specific dish with a long slow bone broth, charred aromatics and a fixed set of garnishes, and it deserves the two hours it takes. Ours is a forty minute weeknight broth built on bought stock, and calling it what it is seems better than borrowing a name it has not earned.

If you want the real versions, eat at the small places around Nicosia's old town and the streets behind Limassol port, and cook from writers who grew up in those kitchens. It is worth your money and it is a short drive.

The short pantry list

Twelve things that will get you through most weeknight cooking in this style. Nearly all of them now turn up in the world foods aisle at Alphamega, Sklavenitis or Papantoniou, and Lidl Cyprus runs an Asian week two or three times a year. All of them are cheaper and better at an Asian grocer.

Light soy sauce
The everyday one. Salty and thin. Not the same as dark soy, which is thicker, sweeter and mostly there for colour. Easy to find here.
Fish sauce
Buy a brand with two or three ingredients on the label. It smells alarming in the bottle and tastes savoury rather than fishy in a finished dish. Stocked in most large supermarkets.
Rice vinegar
Milder than our white or red wine vinegar. Used for brightness at the end rather than for pickling. Cider vinegar cut with a little water is a workable stand-in.
Toasted sesame oil
Dark, used by the teaspoon as a finishing oil. It burns, so it is not a cooking oil. Sold everywhere, including the health food shops.
Shaoxing wine
Chinese cooking wine, and the one thing on this list you may have to hunt for. Dry sherry is the standard substitute; a dry Cypriot white with a pinch of sugar will do at a push. Do not reach for commandaria, it is far too sweet.
Oyster sauce
Thick and savoury. Vegetarian mushroom versions are widely available and very good.
Chilli oil or chilli crisp
A jar of it improves eggs, noodles, dumplings and roast vegetables. Supermarket versions here are thin, so this is worth buying at the grocer.
Fresh ginger
Sold in every supermarket and at the laiki. Buy a large knob and keep it in the freezer, where it grates without stringing.
Garlic and spring onions
Bought weekly, used daily. Cypriot spring onions in spring are better than anything you will find in a packet.
Rice noodles
Dried flat noodles for soups, thin vermicelli for salads. Dried are easy here. Fresh rice noodles are the hard one and are worth a special trip.
Jasmine rice
Buy the biggest bag you have room for. It is dramatically cheaper by the kilo at an Asian grocer than in a supermarket box.
Star anise and white pepper
Two spices that do most of the work in broths. Star anise is in every Cypriot spice rack already, because it goes into afelia country cooking too.

What is easy here, and what is not

Cyprus is a small island at the end of a supply chain, so the gap between a recipe written in London and a shop in Limassol is real. It is almost always a sourcing problem rather than a cooking problem, and almost always solvable.

Easy

Soy sauce, fish sauce, sesame oil, oyster sauce, coconut milk, curry pastes, dried noodles of every shape, jasmine and basmati rice, fresh ginger, garlic, chillies, coriander, spring onions, limes, peanuts, tofu in the chilled aisle of the bigger supermarkets. None of this needs a plan.

Harder, but findable

Shaoxing wine, doubanjiang, gochujang, mirin, dried shiitake, black vinegar, fresh rice noodles, good chilli crisp, wonton and gyoza wrappers, frozen lemongrass and galangal. The Asian grocers in Nicosia and Limassol carry most of these, and the frozen cabinet is usually where the aromatics hide.

Genuinely hard

Fresh Thai basil, shiso, curry leaves, Vietnamese mint, gai lan and choy sum in reliable condition, and fresh yuzu. These come and go, and in July nothing leafy travels well.

Substitutions that actually work

  • Thai basil, missing: ordinary basil plus a couple of fennel seeds crushed into the dish. Not the same plant, but the right direction.
  • Vietnamese mint: the mint from the laiki, used more generously than the recipe says, with extra coriander.
  • Gai lan or choy sum: Cypriot chard stems and leaves, or the tender inner leaves of a spring cabbage. Cook the stems first, the leaves last.
  • Shaoxing wine: dry sherry, or a dry white with a pinch of sugar.
  • Mirin: dry white wine with half a teaspoon of sugar per tablespoon.
  • Rice vinegar: cider vinegar cut one to four with water. Do not use our red wine vinegar, it takes over.
  • Fresh rice noodles: dried flat noodles soaked in just-boiled water rather than boiled, which keeps them slippery instead of soft.

Where to shop

Nicosia has the widest choice, in the streets around the old town where the Chinese, Vietnamese and Sri Lankan shops sit within a few minutes of each other. Limassol's grocers cluster near the port and along the older end of Anexartisias. Both cities also have Indian and Filipino shops that carry a different and often better range of pulses, spices and frozen aromatics. Expect to pay roughly half the supermarket price for soy, noodles, rice and fresh herbs, take cash for the smaller shops, and ask what came in this week rather than working from a list.

Three techniques worth practising

Velveting

Tossing sliced meat with a teaspoon of cornflour, a teaspoon of Shaoxing wine and a little soy before it hits the pan. It takes two minutes and it is why restaurant stir-fried beef is tender and yours is not. The starch forms a thin coating that holds moisture through high heat.

Getting the pan hot enough

A home cooktop produces nothing like the heat of a commercial wok burner. The practical fix is to cook in smaller batches than feels necessary and to accept that a heavy frying pan often beats a wok on an electric or induction hob, because it holds heat better. Do not add cold meat to a pan that has not recovered.

Balancing at the end

Salty, sour, sweet and hot are adjusted in the last thirty seconds, not at the start. Keep fish sauce, lime or vinegar, sugar and chilli within reach and taste before serving. Nearly every dish in this style that tastes flat is missing acid, not salt, which is the same lesson the island teaches with lemon.
