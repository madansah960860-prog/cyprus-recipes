The Wider Mediterranean

Cyprus is already the Eastern Mediterranean, so this page is about everywhere else on the same sea. Italy, Spain, the Levant and North Africa, read from a Limassol kitchen, and what each of them does that we do not.

Same sea, different accent

Nothing on this page needs to be argued into a Cypriot kitchen. The climate is the same, the olive tree is the same, the growing calendar is within a few weeks of ours anywhere on the northern shore. A Sicilian and a Cypriot given the same box of August tomatoes will both know exactly what to do with them.

Where the cuisines part company is in the seasoning and the fat. Cyprus reaches for dried oregano, coriander seed, cinnamon and lemon. Italy reaches for basil, anchovy, chilli and cured pork. Spain reaches for smoked paprika, saffron and sherry vinegar. The Levant reaches for sumac, za'atar, tahini and pomegranate. North Africa reaches for cumin, caraway, preserved lemon, harissa and a cone-shaped lid.

So the useful way to cook this food here is not to import a whole new pantry. It is to buy four or five jars that Cyprus does not already have, and let them redirect ingredients you were going to buy anyway. A tray of August aubergines can go towards a Cypriot moussaka, a Sicilian caponata, a Levantine plate under tahini, or a Moroccan zaalouk, and the difference is about five euro of spices.

The Modern Cypriot page covers the island's own cooking. This one deliberately stays off that ground.

What to add to a Cypriot pantry

Assuming you already have olive oil, lemons, dried oregano, tahini, bulgur and tinned tomatoes, these are the things that unlock the rest of the sea.

Bronze-die dried pasta
Rough and pale rather than smooth and yellow. Sauce clings to it instead of sliding off. This one upgrade changes more pasta dinners than any technique.
Anchovies in oil
The Italian and Provençal savoury base. Two fillets melted into hot oil at the start disappear entirely and make the dish taste of more than it contains.
Smoked paprika, sweet and hot
Spanish pimenton. Add it off the heat or it turns bitter in seconds. It is the difference between a pan of potatoes and patatas bravas.
Saffron
Expensive by the gram and cheap by the dish, because you need a pinch. Soak the threads in a little warm water before they go in so the colour spreads.
Sherry vinegar
Rounder and nuttier than our red wine vinegar. It is the right acid for anything with almonds, paprika or roast peppers in it.
Pomegranate molasses and sumac
Levantine sourness, quite different from lemon. Sumac over grilled meat and sliced onion, molasses whisked into a dressing with olive oil.
Harissa and preserved lemon
North African heat and salt. Both keep for months in the fridge and both rescue plain roast vegetables.
Ras el hanout or baharat
Warm ground blends for slow lamb, chickpeas and rice. Buy small quantities, they fade.
Dried pulses
Chickpeas, brown lentils, cannellini. Better and far cheaper than tinned if you soak the night before, and the cooking water is a stock.
Parmesan or pecorino
Buy a wedge and freeze the rinds for braises. Note that traditional parmesan is made with animal rennet. For a Cypriot equivalent, dried anari grates the same way.

Four coastlines, four ideas worth stealing

Italy: fewer things on the plate

The discipline that is hardest to import here. A Cypriot table wants abundance; an Italian plate wants three ingredients and no distractions. Pasta with garlic, oil and chilli is a whole dinner and does not want tomato added to it. Practise by removing something rather than adding something.

Spain: cooking onions until they are gone

A sofrito is onion, and sometimes pepper and tomato, cooked in olive oil far past the point where a Cypriot cook would call it done. Forty minutes on low, stirred occasionally, until it is jammy and no longer looks like onion. Everything built on top of it tastes sweeter and deeper for it.

The Levant: dress the dish, do not sauce it

Tahini thinned with lemon and ice water, yoghurt with garlic, a spoon of pomegranate molasses. These go under the food or beside it and are made cold in two minutes. They do the same job as a long-cooked sauce for a fraction of the effort, which is why a Levantine table can be assembled so fast.

North Africa: sweet and savoury in the same pot

Apricots with lamb, dates with chicken, honey against cumin and chilli. Cyprus does this in small ways, with cinnamon in minced meat and carob in savoury glazes, but Morocco commits to it. Start with a handful of dried fruit in a slow braise and taste what it does to the salt.

One shop, four dinners

Buy a kilo of aubergines, a kilo of onions, a tin of chickpeas and a bunch of coriander at the laiki and you can cook all four of the above in a week without a second trip. Rotate the jar, not the shopping list.

Buying olive oil when you live next to the mill

Cyprus presses its own oil, and between October and December you can buy it within weeks of the harvest, which is a position most of Europe would envy. That does not make every bottle good, and it is worth knowing what to look at.

Three things on the label. A harvest date, not just a best-before, and ideally inside the last twelve months. A dark glass bottle or a tin, because light destroys oil faster than time does. And the words extra virgin, which is a legal grade with a chemical standard behind it, unlike pure or light, which describe refined oil.

Two bottles is the sensible setup: a workhorse for cooking and a better, greener, more peppery one for finishing. A good Cypriot oil from the Troodos foothills is a fine finishing oil and does not need to be replaced by an imported one. If you want a Tuscan or a Catalan bottle for a specific dish, buy a small one and use it inside two months.

Keep it in a cupboard, not on the counter beside the hob. In a Cypriot summer, a bottle left in the sun on a balcony is finished in a fortnight.

Salt, acid and the fix for flat food

If a dish from anywhere on this sea tastes dull, it is usually short of acid rather than salt. Add a squeeze of lemon or a teaspoon of vinegar and taste again before reaching for the salt. Sherry vinegar for anything Spanish, red wine vinegar for Italian, lemon for everything else. This one habit improves more home cooking than any piece of equipment.
