Barbecued Pork Chops with Charred Pineapple Salsa

By Elena Christodoulou. Published 12/08/2026. Tested four times, twice over charcoal on a foukou and twice on a gas grill.

Sunday in a Cypriot yard usually means the foukou is lit, the charcoal is settling to grey, and somebody is arguing about when to put the meat on. This is the quick version, chops rather than a whole souvla, and a fifteen minute brine does the heavy lifting. It seasons the pork right through and buys you a far wider margin before it dries out, which is exactly what you want over charcoal, where the heat is never quite even.

Buy the right chop

Ask the butcher for loin chops cut 3 cm thick, bone in, about 250 g each. Say kotsi or brizoles and hold your fingers apart to show the thickness; every neighbourhood butcher in Limassol and Nicosia will cut them for you while you wait. The thin chops on foam trays at Alphamega or Lidl Cyprus cook through in about ninety seconds a side, which leaves no time for colour, and they are dry by the time they look appetising. If thick chops are not available, pork neck steaks work the same way and are what most people reach for anyway.

Pork does not need to be cooked to grey any more. Current EU and Cypriot food safety guidance supports serving whole cuts of pork at 63°C after resting, which leaves the middle faintly pink and considerably juicier. Mince and rolled roasts are different and should still be cooked through.

Why this works on charcoal

A foukou is built for souvla: a long, low bed of charcoal and a motor turning meat slowly a good distance above it. Chops want the opposite. Rake the coals into one hot half and leave the other half bare, so you have somewhere to move a chop that is colouring too fast. The brine matters more here than on a gas grill, because charcoal heat surges when fat drips and a brined chop shrugs that off. Lump charcoal from the kiosk burns hotter and cleaner than briquettes and is what most Cypriot households use.

Method

  1. Brine the chops

    Stir the salt and sugar into the cold water until dissolved. Sit the chops in the brine for 15 minutes, no longer. Fifteen minutes seasons the meat without making it taste like ham.

  2. Get the fire right

    On charcoal, light it early and wait until the coals are covered in grey ash with no flame, then rake them to one side. On gas, turn the plate to high and give it a full ten minutes with the lid down. Either way you want around 230°C at the bars. Meat put on a grill that has not properly heated is the main reason pork sticks.

  3. Dry, oil and season

    Lift the chops out, discard the brine and pat them very dry. Rub with olive oil and the cracked pepper. Do not add more salt, the brine has done that.

  4. Char the pineapple first

    Lay the pineapple rings on the hot plate and grill for 3 minutes each side, until they carry dark bar marks and smell sweet. Move them to a board and dice once cool enough to handle.

  5. Grill the chops

    Lay the chops down and leave them alone for 4 minutes. Turn once and give them another 4 minutes. Resist moving them around, the crust needs contact time.

  6. Render the fat edge

    Stand the chops on their fat edge, leaning them against each other, for 2 minutes. This renders the fat and is the step most people skip.

  7. Rest

    Move the chops to a warm plate and rest for 5 minutes, loosely covered. The internal temperature will settle at about 63°C. Resting is not optional here.

  8. Build the salsa and serve

    Toss the diced pineapple with the red pepper, onion, chilli, coriander, lime juice, oil and salt flakes. Taste and adjust the lime. Spoon over the rested chops with the plate juices.

Cook's tips

  • Do not brine for longer than 20 minutes. This is a strong brine at 60 g of salt per litre. Left overnight it will cure the meat and the texture turns bouncy and wet.
  • Cook the pineapple before the pork, not after. Fruit sugars burn onto the plate and the residue will scorch anything that follows. Doing it first also means the salsa can sit and mingle while the pork cooks and rests.
  • Use the fat edge to test your heat. If the fat is not sizzling within ten seconds of touching the bars, the fire is not ready. Take the chops off and wait. Charcoal rewards patience and punishes hurry.
  • Keep a bare patch of grill. Coals flare when fat drips. Somewhere cool to park a chop for thirty seconds is worth more than any technique.
  • Make it in a pan indoors. A heavy cast iron pan over high heat gives almost the same result, minus the smoke that makes it taste like a Sunday. Open a window, and add the pineapple to the same pan afterwards with a knob of butter.

Storage

Cooked pork keeps in an airtight container in the fridge for up to 3 days. Slice it cold for sandwiches rather than reheating, which is the fastest way to make a good chop tough. The salsa keeps for 2 days but loses its crunch, so eat it on day one if you can.

Make ahead

The salsa can be made up to 4 hours ahead without the coriander, which is stirred through at the last minute so it stays green. Pineapple can be charred in the morning and left covered at room temperature. The brine can be mixed the night before and kept in the fridge.

Swaps and variations

  • Peaches instead of pineapple in July and August, when the local ones are heavy and dripping. Char them cut side down for two minutes, no longer.
  • Pomegranate and lemon in October, when the trees are loaded. Seeds, chopped parsley, olive oil and lemon instead of the lime and pineapple entirely.
  • Chicken thighs take the same brine and about 6 minutes a side.
  • No chilli if you are feeding small children. Add a little finely grated ginger instead for lift without heat.
