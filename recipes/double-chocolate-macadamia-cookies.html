Double Chocolate and Macadamia Cookies

By Elena Christodoulou. Published 16/08/2026. Tested six times, because biscuits are fussy and the kitchen was 31 degrees for four of them.

Cocoa in the dough and chopped dark chocolate through it, so they are properly chocolatey rather than just brown. Macadamias are an import here, not a local nut, but they give a soft buttery crunch that earns the shelf space. Troodos walnuts or Cypriot almonds do the job differently and just as well.

Weigh everything, and chill the dough

Biscuits are the recipe where cup measures cause the most trouble. A cup of plain flour can weigh anywhere between 140 g and 175 g depending on whether it was scooped or spooned, and 30 g of extra flour in a batch this size is the difference between a soft cookie and a scone. The cups toggle at the top of this page is there for convenience, but if you own scales, use them today.

Chilling matters for the same reason. Cold butter melts more slowly in the oven, which means the edges set before the middle collapses. Thirty minutes is the minimum. Overnight is better and the flavour deepens noticeably.

Buy macadamias raw rather than roasted and salted. Alphamega and the larger Sklavenitis stores keep them with the baking nuts, usually in small vacuum packs, and they are not cheap: this is an imported nut and it never pretends otherwise. Chop them coarsely so you get whole-ish pieces rather than dust.

Or use what grows here

Cyprus has better nuts than the macadamia budget suggests. Walnuts from the Troodos villages come into the shops from late September, sold in the shell at the laiki and shelled by the bag at the supermarket; they are sharper and more tannic than macadamia and they cut through the cocoa rather than melting into it. Almonds are the other honest swap, and green almonds in spring are a different pleasure altogether, though not one for a biscuit. Swap gram for gram, no other change. If you use walnuts, toast them for 6 minutes at 170°C first and let them cool completely, or their bitterness sits on top of the chocolate instead of behind it.

A Cypriot note. Sea salt from the flats at Larnaca or Limassol, crushed between your fingers over the hot tray, is the finish that makes people ask what you did differently.

Method

  1. Cream the butter and sugars

    Beat the softened butter with the brown and caster sugars for a full 3 minutes, until noticeably paler and lighter. The butter should give when pressed but hold its shape. If it is greasy or melting, the cookies will spread flat.

  2. Add the egg and vanilla

    Beat in the egg and vanilla paste until completely combined, scraping down the bowl once. A cold egg can make the mixture look split, which is why it should sit out for twenty minutes first.

  3. Fold in the dry ingredients

    Sift the flour, cocoa, bicarb soda and salt over the top. Fold with a spatula until only a few floury streaks remain. Stop there. Overworking develops gluten and makes tough biscuits.

  4. Add chocolate and macadamias

    Fold through the chopped chocolate and nuts. Chopping a block rather than using buttons gives you shards and pools of chocolate rather than uniform dots.

  5. Roll and chill

    Roll into 18 balls of roughly 60 g each. Arrange them on a tray and refrigerate for at least 30 minutes, or overnight covered.

  6. Heat the oven

    Heat the oven to 180°C, or 160°C fan-forced. Line two trays with baking paper. Space the dough balls at least 6 cm apart, six to a tray.

  7. Bake for 11 to 12 minutes

    The edges should look set and the centres should still look slightly underdone and puffed. They will not seem ready. Take them out anyway.

  8. Salt and cool on the tray

    Sprinkle with sea salt flakes straight from the oven while the surface is still soft. Leave on the tray for 10 minutes to finish setting, then move to a rack. This carry-over cooking is what gives the fudgy middle.

Cook's tips

  • Bang the tray once at 9 minutes. Lift it 5 cm and drop it flat on the oven shelf. It knocks the air out of the risen middle and gives the ridged, crinkled top you see on good bakery cookies.
  • Fan-forced runs hotter than the dial says. Most domestic ovens are 10 to 20°C out, and 180°C conventional is roughly gas mark 4. If your first tray browns too fast on the base, drop the temperature by another 10°C and move the shelf up one notch.
  • Summer dough needs more fridge. From June to September a Cypriot kitchen sits well above 28°C and butter starts soft. Chill the rolled balls for a full hour and bake them straight from cold.
  • Do not use self-raising flour. The bicarb soda is doing a specific job with the brown sugar here, and self-raising flour will add baking powder on top of it. The result is cakey and pale.
  • Bake one tray at a time. Two trays at once in a domestic oven gives you one good tray and one uneven one. If you must, swap them at the halfway mark.

Storage

Store in an airtight container at room temperature for up to 4 days. They are best on day one and still very good on day two. Do not refrigerate baked cookies, the fridge dries them out.

Make ahead and freezing

The rolled dough balls freeze beautifully for up to 3 months. Freeze them solid on a tray, then transfer to a bag. Bake from frozen at the same temperature, adding 2 to 3 minutes. This is the single most useful thing in this recipe, because it turns a batch into fresh biscuits whenever you want two, not eighteen.

Baked cookies also freeze for 2 months. Warm them for 4 minutes at 150°C to bring them back.

Allergens

Contains gluten, dairy, egg and tree nuts (macadamia). Chocolate may contain traces of soy and milk depending on the brand. Read the packaging on the products in your own kitchen. If you are cooking for someone with a nut allergy, leave the macadamias out entirely rather than substituting, and be aware of cross contamination in shared equipment.

Questions people ask about this recipe

Three usual causes. The butter was too soft or partly melted, the dough was not chilled, or there was not enough flour because it was measured by volume rather than weight. Chilling fixes most of it. Troodos walnuts are the local answer and the one we reach for from October, toasted first so they are not bitter. Almonds work too, and pecans are the closest in softness. Hazelnuts, toasted and skinned, are excellent with the cocoa.
