Kitchen Basics & Conversions

Cyprus cooks in grams, millilitres and degrees Celsius, like the rest of the EU. The trouble starts when a recipe arrives from somewhere that does not. These are the numbers we use, the traps worth knowing, and the equipment a Cypriot kitchen actually needs.

Why we write recipes in grams

Every recipe on this site is written in metric weight first. A gram is a gram in Limassol, in Lisbon and in Larnaca. A cup is not: it depends on whose cup you own and how you filled it.

The metric cup is 250 ml, and that is the cup this site means whenever it says cup. It is the standard across the EU, and it is what the Cups toggle in the utility bar on every recipe page gives you.

Two other cups are still in circulation and both will quietly change your baking. The US cup is 240 ml, four per cent smaller. Across a single cup of milk that is invisible. Across three cups in a cake batter it is most of a quarter cup of missing liquid. The imperial cup is 284 ml, which turns up in older British and Commonwealth cookbooks and is fourteen per cent larger than ours. That one is big enough to break a recipe on its own.

Spoons are simpler than people fear. A tablespoon is 15 ml, which is three teaspoons, in Cyprus, across the EU, in the United Kingdom and in the United States. A teaspoon is 5 ml everywhere. The one exception you may still meet is a 20 ml tablespoon in some older southern-hemisphere books, which holds four teaspoons rather than three.

The simple rule

If a recipe comes from abroad and measures anything powerful in cups — flour, sugar, or a liquid you cannot taste and adjust later — find the gram weight instead. Most good sites publish both now. If they do not, weigh using the table further down this page and stop thinking about it.

Oven temperatures

Fan ovens circulate hot air, so they cook faster and more evenly than static ones. The standard adjustment is to set a fan oven 20°C lower than a conventional recipe temperature. Every recipe on this site gives both, and the gas mark for anyone cooking on bottled gas.

Three practical notes. Most domestic ovens sit 10 to 20°C away from the temperature on the dial, and the error is rarely consistent across the range. An oven thermometer costs about €10 at Superhome Center and tells you what your oven actually does. Second, ovens are hotter at the back and near the top, so rotate trays halfway through anything that needs even colour.

Third, and this matters in Cyprus: a great many flats here have a small oven, often 45 to 50 cm wide and sometimes built into a gas hob. Small cavities heat unevenly, the walls are close to the food, and a full tapsi can block the airflow entirely. Use the middle shelf, leave a hand's width of clearance around the tin, and give it a genuine twenty minutes to preheat before anything goes in.

The Cypriot pantry

Keep these seven things in the house and most of the recipes on this site are a short shop away. None of them are expensive, and all of them are on the shelf in any Alphamega or Lidl Cyprus.

Halloumi and anari

Halloumi keeps for weeks in its brine and squeaks when it is fresh. Buy the one with goat and sheep milk high on the label if you want it to taste of something. Anari is what is made from the whey afterwards: soft and mild fresh, or dried and salted for grating over pasta. Fresh anari wants eating within days.

Pourgouri and pulses

Coarse pourgouri for pilafi, fine for salads. Beside it keep dried louvi, chickpeas and red lentils. This is the backbone of Cypriot everyday cooking and of every nistisimo meal, and a kilo costs less than a coffee.

Tahini and carob syrup

Tahini goes into tahinopites, into dressings, and straight onto bread. Charoupomelo, the carob syrup pressed from the pods the island once exported by the shipload, is darker and less sweet than honey. Stir it into yoghurt, or use it where a recipe wants treacle.

Dried oregano and wild thyme

Buy it on the stalk from the laiki or a village stall, not ground in a jar. Crush it between your palms as it goes in so the warmth releases the oil. It is the difference between souvla that tastes Cypriot and souvla that tastes of nothing.

Cyprus sea salt

Coarse flakes for finishing, fine for cooking. Salt from the island's own pans has a clean, bright edge to it. Keep a small bowl of the coarse next to the hob and use your fingers, which is more accurate than any spoon.

Olive oil, two bottles

One everyday bottle for cooking, and one good cold-pressed oil from a village press for finishing. In November, when the presses are running, buy the year's oil in a tin and decant it. Keep both away from the light.

Equipment worth owning

Short list, in the order we would buy it. None of it is essential to cook well, and a great deal of very good food has been made in Cypriot kitchens without any of it. Superhome Center (superhome.com.cy) carries the grills, tins and hardware; Stephanis (stephanis.com.cy) is the place for scales, thermometers and anything with a plug.

  1. Digital scales

    Around €15. Fixes more baking problems than any other purchase. Look for 1 g increments and a tare button.

  2. A tapsi

    The round or rectangular roasting tin that does most of the work in a Cypriot kitchen. Everything from kleftiko to a tray of potatoes with lemon and oregano. Buy heavy: thin tins buckle and pool the juices in one corner.

  3. A foukou

    The long charcoal grill with the turning spit that souvla is cooked on. It is a weekend machine, not a weeknight one, but nothing else produces that meat. A basic one is around €80 to €150, and the rotisserie motor is worth having.

  4. A briki

    The small long-handled pot for Cypriot coffee. A two-cup and a four-cup cover most situations. A few euro each, and they last a lifetime.

  5. An instant read thermometer

    Around €25 to €35. Takes the guesswork out of chicken, pork and fish, and stops you overcooking an expensive cut.

  6. A heavy pan for the hob

    Most Cypriot flats cook on the hob far more than in the oven, especially in July when nobody turns the oven on. One good heavy pan outlasts five thin ones and browns properly.

  7. A sharp knife and a steel

    A blunt knife is more dangerous than a sharp one because it slips. Hone weekly and get it properly sharpened twice a year.

Food safety basics

General guidance only. For current official advice see the Cyprus Ministry of Health and the EU food information rules under Regulation (EU) 1169/2011.

  • Keep the fridge at or below 5°C and the freezer at or below minus 18°C.
  • Cool cooked food quickly and refrigerate within two hours. In a Cypriot summer, one hour.
  • Thaw frozen food in the fridge, never on the bench in August.
  • Reheat leftovers until steaming hot all the way through, and only reheat once.
  • Keep raw meat below and separate from ready to eat food in the fridge.
  • Wash hands, boards and knives between raw meat and anything eaten raw.
  • Food left out at a long meze table is on a clock. Two hours in the shade, one in the heat.
