The Cyprus Seasonal Produce Guide

Cyprus sits in the northern hemisphere, at the eastern end of the Mediterranean. Summer runs from June to August and is long, dry and fierce. Winter is December to February, mild enough on the coast to pick oranges in shirtsleeves and cold enough on Troodos for snow. This is what grows here, month by month.

How to use this guide

The island is small and its growing seasons are not. The coastal plains around Limassol, Larnaca and Paphos run warm and early. The Troodos villages sit a thousand metres up and run several weeks behind, which is why Kyperounta cherries arrive in June when the lowland apricots are already finishing. The Kokkinochoria, the red-soil villages near Paralimni, grow the potatoes that leave the island in crates.

The most honest signal is price. When something is truly in season here, it is stacked in crates at the laiki and it is cheap. If aubergines are €4 a kilo in January, that is the market telling you to cook cauliflower and kolokasi instead. Eating with the season is the simplest way to spend less and eat better, because a tomato that ripened in the July sun and one trucked in during February are not the same vegetable.

One Cypriot habit worth copying: buy the glut and preserve it. Tomatoes become passata in August. Bitter oranges become glyko tou koutaliou in February. Walnuts go into syrup in October. The year is a rhythm of doing something with the excess while it is cheap.

Frozen is not a compromise for everything. Peas, spinach, broad beans and okra are frozen within hours of picking, so out of season they usually beat tired fresh produce on both taste and price.

The four seasons, northern hemisphere

If you have arrived from a recipe site written for the southern hemisphere, invert everything you know. Here, December is cold and July is not.

Spring, March to May

Vegetables. Globe artichokes, broad beans (koukia), agrelia — the wild asparagus people go hunting for along the field edges in March — fresh green garlic, spring onions, peas, the first courgettes, and armfuls of wild greens.

Fruit. Strawberries, loquats (mespila), green almonds eaten whole with salt, the last of the bitter oranges, early apricots in May.

From the sea. Red mullet, calamari, sardines.

Cook. A green salad built on the first tender leaves, ricotta hotcakes with strawberries.

Summer, June to August

Vegetables. Tomatoes at their peak, cucumbers, aubergines, courgettes, okra (bamies), peppers, Cyprus potatoes, fresh black-eyed beans for louvi.

Fruit. Watermelon, melons, figs, grapes from July, peaches, plums, and Kyperounta cherries, which have roughly three weeks in June and are worth the drive up the mountain.

From the sea. Octopus, picarel, sea bream. Most cooking moves outdoors to the foukou after dark.

Cook. Tomato bruschetta, grilled corn with chilli butter, a platter of watermelon, figs and halloumi.

Autumn, September to November

Vegetables. Sweet potatoes, pumpkin, the last aubergines and peppers, leeks, and kolokasi as it starts to come up in November.

Fruit. The grape harvest through September, when the villages make zivania and commandaria and the air smells of must. Then pomegranates, quinces, prickly pears, Troodos walnuts, carobs, and in November the olives — the month the presses run day and night and everyone turns up with crates.

From the sea. Grouper, sea bass, bogue.

Cook. Roast pumpkin soup, pumpkin and chickpea salad with rocket.

Winter, December to February

Vegetables. Cauliflower, cabbage, kolokasi (taro), spinach, leeks, celery, late artichokes, and wild greens picked off the hillsides after the first proper rain.

Fruit. Citrus, which is the whole point of a Cypriot winter: oranges, mandarins, lemons and the bitter oranges that become spoon sweets. Paphos avocados come into their own now, cheap and buttery, and the island eats them by the kilo.

From the sea. Sea bream, red mullet, cuttlefish.

Cook. A long slow ragù, chicken soup with plenty of lemon.

Five dates worth putting in the diary

The Cypriot food year turns on a handful of short windows. Miss them and you wait twelve months.

  • MarAgrelia season. Wild asparagus along the field edges, eaten with eggs.
  • JunKyperounta cherries. Three weeks, up in the Troodos villages.
  • SepThe grape harvest, and the stills working for zivania.
  • NovOlive pressing. Green, peppery oil straight from the mill.
  • DecCitrus and Paphos avocados, both at their cheapest.

Month by month

Best value and best quality at the laiki and in the Cypriot supermarkets. Mountain villages usually run two to four weeks behind the coast, and a hot spring pulls everything forward.

Growing seasons shift with the weather in any given year, and vary between the Paphos coast, the Mesaoria plain and the Troodos villages. Treat this as a starting point and let the price at the laiki be the final word.

Buying well, wherever you shop

The laiki
The weekly street market, and the best value on the island for anything genuinely in season. Growers sell what they picked that week, prices drop in the last hour, and if you ask what is good today you will get an honest answer, because they want you back next Saturday.
Alphamega, Lidl Cyprus, Sklavenitis and Papantoniou
Consistent, air-conditioned and open late. Lidl is usually cheapest on staples, dairy and frozen. Check the country of origin label: plenty of what sits beside the Cypriot produce has been flown in.
Village stalls and the roadside
Oranges outside Paphos, potatoes in the Kokkinochoria, walnuts and cherries on the Troodos road. Cash, no packaging, and the shortest distance between a tree and your kitchen.
The butcher and the fish counter
Better value on cheaper cuts, and they will trim and cut for you. For fish, ask what came in this morning rather than arriving with a fixed list.

Ask before you buy

Two questions get you most of the way: pou einai apo? — where is it from — and what is good today. Cypriot growers answer both straight, and the answer often changes what you cook.

Storing produce so it lasts

  • Tomatoes never go in the fridge. Below about 12°C they lose flavour and turn mealy. Keep them on the bench, stem side down, out of the afternoon sun.
  • Herbs keep like flowers: stems in a glass of water, a bag over the top, in the fridge. Basil is the exception and stays on the bench.
  • Dried oregano and wild thyme keep best on the stalk, in a paper bag in a dark cupboard. Crush between your palms as you use them.
  • Potatoes and onions in a dark cupboard, but not together. Onions make potatoes sprout.
  • Halloumi stays in its brine in the fridge and only improves. Anari is the opposite and wants eating within days.
  • Wild greens washed, spun dry and rolled in a clean tea towel in the fridge. They will hold most of a week.
  • Watermelon and melons whole on the bench until cut, then covered in the fridge. Cold whole melon loses its perfume.

Cook with what is in season
