About Meraki Table

A Cypriot recipe site written by three people in one home kitchen in Limassol. Everything is cooked before it is published, measured in grams and millilitres, and paid for in ways we are willing to list.

What meraki means

Meraki. You put your soul into the thing you are making, and it shows in the result.

There is no clean English translation. A grandmother folding koupepia one at a time on a Sunday morning has meraki. So does the man who has been turning souvla over the same coals for thirty years and still watches every piece. It is not perfectionism and it is not fuss. It is care that you can taste.

We named the site after the word because it sets the standard we are held to. If a recipe is here, someone stood at a cooker in Limassol and cooked it properly, more than once, then wrote down what actually happened rather than what was supposed to happen.

The three of us

Elena Christodoulou, founder and recipe developer

Elena grew up between Limassol and a village above Pissouri, where her grandmother cooked on a wood oven twice a week and fed whoever turned up. She cooked professionally for twelve years, mostly in small Mediterranean kitchens, and started writing recipes down properly because friends kept asking and her replies kept turning into essays. Why the oil has to be hot before the onion goes in. Why you salt an aubergine and then actually wait. What to do when the avgolemono looks like it has split and is not yet lost.

Restaurant work taught her technique and speed. It did not teach her how to feed five people on a Tuesday with one oven, one sink and nobody to wash up but herself. That gap is the site.

Andreas Georgiou, photographer and kitchen tester

Andreas shoots every photograph on the site and cooks the second test of every recipe, which is the one that matters. He follows the written method exactly as a stranger would, with no phone call to Elena allowed. If he cannot get the same result from the words on the page, the words on the page are wrong. Roughly one recipe in four goes back to the cooker because of him. He shoots in daylight, on the same bench the food was cooked on, and nothing here has been styled with anything you could not eat.

Marina Savva, editor, conversions and nutrition

Marina edits every word before it goes up and checks every number twice: weights, volumes, tin sizes, oven temperatures, timings and the nutrition estimates. She is the reason a cup on this site is always 250 ml, the reason fan temperatures sit beside conventional ones, and the reason nothing here claims to be healing, detoxifying or slimming. She also answers most of the email.

What you can expect here

  • Grams and millilitres by default, with a metric 250 ml cup toggle on every recipe.
  • Cypriot and EU ingredient names, and shopping notes that assume you buy on the island.
  • Oven temperatures in °C with the fan equivalent and the gas mark beside them.
  • Prep, cook and total times, servings, difficulty, allergen tags, storage and make-ahead notes on every recipe.
  • No childhood memoir above the ingredients. The recipe is near the top and there is a jump link in the bar.

How a recipe gets tested

Every recipe is cooked at least twice before it is published, and most are cooked three or four times. All of that happens in a home kitchen in Limassol, not a studio and not a professional line.

  1. Cooked in a domestic kitchen

    A standard 60 cm electric fan oven, a four-burner gas hob, one gas grill on the balcony and a cheap stick blender. No combi oven, no blast chiller, no salamander. If a recipe needs a stand mixer or a food processor we say so, and we say what to do if you do not own one.

  2. Cooked twice, by two people

    Elena develops it. Andreas then cooks it from the written words alone, without asking her anything. If his version is not the same dish, the method is rewritten and cooked again.

  3. Bought locally, at ordinary prices

    Ingredients come from the supermarkets and markets any household in Cyprus uses, at whatever the shelf price is that week. Nothing is ordered from a specialist supplier abroad and then quietly presented as normal.

  4. Checked, then checked again

    Marina re-weighs, re-measures and re-times. Our oven runs about 12 °C cool at 200 °C. We correct for it during testing and we mention it, because yours is almost certainly out too and an oven thermometer costs about €10.

  5. Failures stay unpublished

    There are a dozen or so recipes in a folder that did not work, including three attempts at a gluten-free flaouna that all collapsed in the middle. They are not on the site and they are not going on the site.

Summer testing has its own rule. Between June and August a Limassol kitchen sits near 32 °C, dough proves at roughly double speed and butter will not hold a shape. Anything that behaves differently in that heat is cooked again in winter, and the recipe tells you what to watch for.

Where we shop, and why

A recipe is only as honest as the shopping behind it. This is where the ingredients in our photographs actually come from.

The laiki, the weekly street market. First stop most weeks. Vegetables cost less, they were picked closer to the day you are cooking, and you can ask the grower what is about to finish. In April that means artichokes, broad beans, agrelia and green almonds. In October it is pomegranates, quinces, carobs and the first walnuts. The seasonal notes on this site are written from what was on those tables that month, not copied from a produce chart.

Alphamega, Lidl Cyprus, Sklavenitis and Papantoniou. The weekly shop. Flour, dairy, tinned goods, halloumi, anari, olive oil, pourgouri and trachanas. If a recipe calls for something, we have checked that at least one of these four stocks it. When only one of them does, we name it.

The butcher, not the chilled aisle. Pork for afelia and sheftalia, lamb on the bone, and anything that needs cutting a particular way. A butcher will trim, mince or butterfly for you at no extra cost, and the result is better than a tray.

The villages, when the drive is worth it. Olive oil from a press we have stood in, carob syrup from Anogyra, thyme honey from the Troodos foothills, walnuts in autumn. We buy these directly because the difference is real, and we say so rather than pretending the supermarket version is identical.

Prices in recipe notes are what we paid in Limassol, in euro, in the month of testing. They drift. Treat them as a guide to whether something is cheap right now, not as a quote.

A note on halloumi

Halloumi is a protected designation of origin product, and the real thing is made in Cyprus, usually from sheep and goat milk with some cow milk. It squeaks, it holds its shape in a hot pan, and it is salty enough that you should taste before seasoning anything it goes into.

If you are cooking these recipes off the island and cannot find it, the closest behaviour comes from Greek halloumi-style cheese, Turkish hellim or a firm queso para freír. Paneer will not brown the same way and mozzarella simply melts. There is more on this in the FAQ.

Tested for the island

  • Hob timings assume gas, because most Cypriot kitchens have it.
  • Bread and pastry recipes note what changes above 30 °C.
  • Yoghurt, lemons and decent olive oil are assumed to be in the fridge or on the counter. They usually are.
  • Where a dish has a nistisimo version for the fasting weeks, it is written into the recipe rather than left to you.

How this site is funded

The recipes are free and there is no paywall on any cooking content. Running the site still costs money, so here is exactly where that money comes from. The full detail is on our advertising and affiliate disclosure page.

  1. Display advertising

    We sell advertising space through partners including Google. Ads are labelled, kept below the main content, and never placed over a recipe, a button or the navigation. There are no ads on our legal pages or the error page.

  2. Affiliate commissions

    Some links to kitchen equipment and pantry ingredients earn a small commission if you buy through them. Every one of those links carries a disclosure beside it, not only in the footer, and the price you pay is the same either way.

  3. Paid cookbooks and meal plan bundles

    We sell downloadable PDF cookbooks and seasonal meal-plan bundles priced in euro, currently from €9.00. Buying one is optional and takes nothing away from the free site.

  4. Optional membership

    Membership is €4.50 a month for ad-free browsing, printable recipe cards and saved collections. Everything a member sees is also on the free site, with ads.

  5. Sponsored and brand-partnered recipes

    Occasionally a brand pays us to develop a recipe. Those pages carry a sponsorship label at the very top, above the recipe, as EU and Cypriot consumer law require and as the Consumer Protection Service expects. We keep control of the method and the opinions.

  6. Newsletter sponsorship

    The Thursday email sometimes carries one sponsor, clearly labelled inside the email. Rates are on the work with us page.

What we refuse to do

  • No untested recipes. Nothing is published that has not been cooked in this kitchen at least twice. Nothing is generated and posted unread, and we do not rewrite someone else's recipe and call it tested.
  • No padded intros. No eight hundred words about a holiday before the ingredient list. Context stays when it changes how you cook the dish, and goes when it does not.
  • No hidden sponsorship. If someone paid for a page, the label sits at the top of it, above the recipe, in a box you cannot miss. Not a hashtag, not a line at the end, not the footer.
  • No health or medical claims. We are cooks, not dietitians or doctors. Nothing here is nutritional advice, and we do not describe food as healing, detoxifying, slimming or therapeutic. For a dietary or medical need, speak to a qualified professional.
  • No invented nutrition figures presented as fact. Nutrition panels are estimates and are labelled as estimates on every recipe.
  • No fake reviews or fabricated ratings. Ratings come from real readers. Where a figure is a placeholder during development, it is marked in the page source.
  • No superlatives we cannot back up. You will not find best ever, number one or guaranteed here. We would rather tell you what the dish actually tastes like.

Corrections and requests

If a recipe does not work for you, tell us. Say what happened, what oven you used and where it went wrong, and we will cook it again. Genuine errors are corrected on the page and the change is noted, not quietly edited away.

We also keep a list of requests and work through it when planning the next round of testing. Requests for allergen-friendly or nistisima versions of existing recipes are the most useful, because those take the most cooking and we would rather spend that time on something people are actually waiting for.
