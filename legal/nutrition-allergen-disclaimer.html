Nutrition & Allergen Disclaimer

Nutrition figures on this site are estimates. Allergen tags are a starting point, not a clearance. Neither is a substitute for reading the packaging in your own kitchen or speaking to a qualified health professional.

Last updated 14 September 2026. Version 2.0.

Please note. This legal copy is a starting template and must be reviewed by a Cyprus-qualified lawyer before launch. It is not legal, medical or dietary advice.

1. Nutrition information is an estimate

Where a recipe on this site shows a nutrition panel, those figures are estimates only. They are calculated from the ingredients as listed and are given for general information.

A recipe published on a website is not a packaged food. Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011 on the provision of food information to consumers sets the rules for the mandatory nutrition declaration on food sold to you in a packet, and it is that declaration, on the packet in your hand, that carries legal weight. Our panel does not. We follow the same units and order the Regulation uses, per 100 g and per portion where useful, energy in kilojoules and kilocalories, then fat, of which saturates, carbohydrate, of which sugars, protein and salt, because a familiar shape is easier to read. Following the shape is not the same as being a legal declaration, and ours is not one.

Estimates will differ from what you actually eat, sometimes substantially, because:

  • brands vary. Two tins of the same product can differ in salt, sugar and fat;
  • produce varies. A large onion and a small onion are both one onion;
  • portions vary. A recipe that serves four serves four different amounts depending on who is at the table;
  • substitutions change everything, and we encourage substitutions;
  • optional ingredients, garnishes, oil left in the pan and anything served alongside are generally not counted;
  • cooking itself changes composition, particularly where fat renders out or liquid evaporates;
  • even the reference tables behind any calculation carry their own tolerances. Article 31 of the Regulation allows declared values to be averages, and averages are what these are.

Do not rely on these figures for medical, clinical or therapeutic purposes, for managing a diagnosed condition such as diabetes or coeliac disease, or for anything where precision matters. Speak to your doctor or a registered clinical dietitian instead.

During site development, some nutrition values on this site are placeholders and are marked as such in the page source. Every figure must be replaced with a verified analysis before launch.

2. Allergen information and the 14 declarable allergens

Recipes on this site flag the allergens we are aware of in the recipe as written. The list we work from is Annex II to Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011, which names the fourteen substances that must be declared on food across the EU, Cyprus included:

Two that catch people out in Cypriot cooking

  • Halloumi and anari are dairy. Both are milk products and both are declarable milk allergens. Halloumi is traditionally a mix of goat's, sheep's and sometimes cow's milk, and anari is the fresh whey cheese made from what is left after the halloumi. Neither is lactose-free, neither is safe for a milk allergy, and goat's or sheep's milk is not a safe substitute for someone allergic to cow's milk protein. They appear in a great many recipes on this site, sometimes only as a grating at the end, so read the ingredient list rather than the title.
  • Sesame is everywhere here, and tahini is sesame. Tahini is nothing but ground sesame seed, and it runs through this cuisine: in tahinopita, in halva, stirred into dips, in the Lenten cooking, and as a sesame crust on daktyla, koulouria and village loaves. If you are cooking for someone with a sesame allergy, treat every bread, every sweet and every dip as suspect until you have read the label, and be aware that sesame seeds travel across a kitchen and stick to boards, trays and the inside of a bread bin.

Our allergen tags are a starting point for your own checking, not a clearance. We cannot know:

  • which brands you buy, or what those brands have reformulated since we tested;
  • what precautionary statement appears on your packaging, such as may contain traces. Precautionary labelling is voluntary in the EU and is not standardised, so its absence proves nothing;
  • what else is in your kitchen, on your boards, in your oil or on your equipment;
  • what you bought loose at the laiki or over a counter, where the allergen information is given to you verbally or on a sign rather than on a packet;
  • what substitutions you have made.

Always read the label on every product you use, every time. Under Article 21 of the Regulation, allergens must be emphasised in the ingredients list on packaged food, usually in bold. Manufacturers reformulate without warning, and the packet in your hand is the only reliable source.

3. If you are cooking for someone with an allergy

Food allergy can be life threatening. If you are cooking for someone with a diagnosed allergy or intolerance:

  • ask them what they need, rather than assuming from a tag on a website;
  • read every label yourself, including on staples you have used a hundred times;
  • for food sold loose or at a counter, ask the seller directly. EU rules require allergen information to be available for non-prepacked food too, even where it is not printed on a packet;
  • watch for cross contamination from shared boards, utensils, toasters, fryers, oil and the grill;
  • follow any management plan they have been given by their doctor or allergy specialist, and know where the adrenaline auto-injector is;
  • if in doubt, do not serve it.

For advice about food allergy in Cyprus, speak to your doctor, a clinical immunologist or allergist, or a registered clinical dietitian. The European Academy of Allergy and Clinical Immunology and national patient organisations publish resources for households and for schools.

4. We make no health or medical claims

We are cooks, not dietitians, nutritionists or medical practitioners. Nothing on this site is dietary advice, nutritional advice or medical advice, and nothing here should be used to diagnose, treat, cure or prevent any condition.

You will not find claims on this site that a food or recipe is healthy in a therapeutic sense, that it will help you lose weight, that it detoxifies anything, that it boosts immunity, or that it treats or prevents illness. Regulation (EC) No 1924/2006 permits only authorised nutrition and health claims on food in the EU, and prohibits claims that a food prevents, treats or cures disease altogether. We do not make those claims because we are not qualified to, because the law does not allow them, and because for the most part they are not true.

Where we describe a dish as light, fresh, rich or filling, we are describing how it tastes and eats, not making a nutritional statement.

6. Food safety

Cooking times and temperatures in our recipes are a guide, based on testing in one home kitchen in Limassol. Your equipment will differ. Use the visual and temperature cues given in each recipe, and make sure food is cooked through before you serve it.

General food safety guidance appears on our kitchen basics page. For authoritative advice, see the European Food Safety Authority and, in Cyprus, the Food Safety Council and the relevant services of the Ministry of Health.

Some groups, including people who are pregnant, very young children, older adults and people who are immunocompromised, are advised to avoid certain foods, such as raw or undercooked eggs, raw seafood, unpasteurised milk and some soft cheeses. Summer heat in Cyprus is its own hazard: food left on a table outdoors in July is not in a safe temperature range for long. If any of this applies to you, follow current advice from your doctor or the health authorities rather than a recipe website.

7. Alcohol

A small number of recipes include alcohol as an ingredient, such as wine in a braise, commandaria in a syrup or a splash of zivania. Not all of it cooks off. Where a recipe contains alcohol we say so, and we give a substitution where one works. Do not serve those dishes to anyone avoiding alcohol without making the substitution. Note also that wine and commandaria commonly carry sulphites above the declarable threshold.
